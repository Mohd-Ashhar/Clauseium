## Why a vendor agreement template is different from an NDA or MSA

Indian counsel treat vendor agreements as the workhorse of the procurement
function: they cover everything from a one-off SaaS subscription to a
five-year managed-services arrangement. The drafting question is not "do we
need confidentiality clauses?" — those go in by default — but "what are the
operational guardrails when the vendor underperforms?" That puts the
emphasis on Service Levels, remedies short of termination, and a clean
exit framework. An NDA controls information flow before contracting. A
[mutual NDA template](/resources/templates/nda-template-india) handles the
pre-contract phase. A vendor agreement governs the live commercial
relationship — typically over multiple years, with changing scope and
escalating spend.

The template is built around three drafting principles that Indian
in-house counsel have validated through actual disputes:

1. **Specificity wins over comprehensiveness.** A 60-page vendor agreement
   that copies every clause from a US template loses in arbitration when
   the parties disagree on what "best efforts" means. The template uses
   measurable SLA thresholds, dollar-defined liquidated damages, and
   explicit acceptance criteria.
2. **Operational clauses go in schedules, not the body.** Pricing, SLAs,
   security requirements, and the Statement of Work belong in numbered
   schedules so they can be amended by the operations teams without legal
   re-negotiating the master.
3. **Indian-law-specific clauses are non-negotiable.** Stamp duty, MSME
   compliance, DPDP carve-outs, and arbitration seat selection cannot be
   imported wholesale from a foreign template. The template embeds the
   Indian-specific defaults inline and flags where Indian counsel must
   adapt.

## What this template covers

The agreement is structured as a **master vendor agreement** that anticipates
multiple SOWs over the contract term. Operative clauses include:

- **Engagement and SOW framework** — defines that all work is governed by
  signed Statements of Work referencing the master, so adding new scope
  doesn't require re-papering the legal terms.
- **Service levels and credits** — measurable thresholds (uptime, response
  time, defect resolution) with monthly service credits as the
  pre-litigation remedy. Mirrors the structure Indian courts have upheld
  in technology vendor disputes.
- **Pricing and invoicing** — supports fixed-fee, time-and-materials, and
  milestone billing. Includes the MSME 45-day payment fallback that
  overrides longer terms under
  <LegalCitation statute="Micro, Small and Medium Enterprises Development Act, 2006" section="15" text="Where any supplier supplies goods or renders services to any buyer, the buyer shall make payment on or before the date agreed upon between him and the supplier in writing or, where there is no agreement, before the appointed day, which shall not exceed 45 days." />.
- **Acceptance and warranties** — 30-day acceptance window with deemed
  acceptance unless the buyer flags specific defects. Implied warranties
  under
  <LegalCitation statute="Sale of Goods Act, 1930" section="16" text="Where the buyer makes known to the seller the particular purpose for which the goods are required, there is an implied condition that the goods shall be reasonably fit for such purpose." />
  expressly preserved.
- **Intellectual property** — vendor retains IP in pre-existing materials;
  buyer owns deliverables outright on payment. Optional licence-back for
  vendor's continued use of buyer customisations is bracketed.
- **Indemnification** — vendor indemnifies for IP infringement, breach of
  confidentiality, and DPDP violations. See the
  [indemnification clause deep-dive](/resources/clauses/indemnification-clause-india)
  for the underlying drafting framework.
- **Limitation of liability** — capped at 12 months of fees with the
  standard carve-outs (IP, confidentiality, gross negligence, fraud, DPDP).
- **Termination** — for convenience (30 days), for cause (15 days post-cure
  notice), and for insolvency (immediate).
- **Dispute resolution** — institutional arbitration under MCIA, DIAC, or
  ICC India rules, seated in India, conducted in English.

## A practical example

Consider a Bengaluru-based fintech, Pivot Payments, onboarding a
managed-services vendor, NorthStar IT, for 18 months of platform support.
Pivot's procurement team negotiated commercials at ₹2 crore annual run-rate
with quarterly renewals. The template gives Pivot's counsel a clean way to
operationalise that deal:

- The **master agreement** is signed once, governs the relationship for
  three years.
- The **Statement of Work** (Schedule A) defines NorthStar's specific
  deliverables: 24×7 platform support, 99.5% uptime, P1 incident response
  in 30 minutes.
- The **Pricing Schedule** (Schedule B) sets ₹2 crore annual fees billed
  monthly, with quarterly true-up for over-and-under-utilisation.
- The **Service Level Schedule** (Schedule C) defines credits: 5% of
  monthly fees for missing uptime, 10% for missed P1 response.
- The **Data Processing Schedule** (Schedule D) makes NorthStar a
  processor under the DPDP Act, with 48-hour breach notification.

When Pivot wants to add a new workstream six months in, the operations
teams sign a new SOW under the same master — no new legal review required.

## Indemnification: the clause that actually matters

The indemnification clause is the single most-litigated provision in
Indian vendor agreements. The template uses a layered approach. The
vendor indemnifies for:

1. **Third-party IP infringement** by the deliverables — uncapped, with
   the vendor's right to procure rights, modify the work, or refund.
2. **Breach of confidentiality** — capped at 24 months of fees (the
   "super-cap" exception to the general 12-month cap).
3. **Breach of DPDP obligations** — uncapped where regulator penalties or
   data principal claims arise from the vendor's failure to meet
   processor obligations under
   <LegalCitation statute="Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023" section="8" text="Section 8 imposes obligations on data fiduciaries and, by Section 8(5), requires them to enter into a valid contract with any data processor." />.
4. **Gross negligence and wilful misconduct** — uncapped.

Buyer-side counsel often push for additional indemnities: regulatory
investigations, employment claims by vendor staff, environmental
violations. The template flags these as bracketed options in Clause 12
and provides commentary on when to insist on them.

## DPDP: the data processing schedule

Most vendor relationships involve some processing of personal data — even
a payroll vendor handles employee data, even an analytics vendor sees
hashed user identifiers. The template's Schedule D operationalises the
processor obligations under
<LegalCitation statute="Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023" section="8" text="Section 8(5) requires the data fiduciary to enter into a valid contract with the data processor obligating the processor to process personal data only on the fiduciary's instructions." />.
For a deeper walkthrough of these obligations, see the
[DPDP compliance guide](/resources/dpdp/dpdp-compliance-guide).

The schedule covers:

| Obligation                       | Schedule clause | Default                     |
| -------------------------------- | --------------- | --------------------------- |
| Process only on instruction      | Clause 1        | Yes; documented instructions |
| Security measures                | Clause 3        | ISO 27001 + Annex A controls |
| Sub-processor approval           | Clause 6        | General + 30-day notice     |
| Personal data breach notification | Clause 4       | 48 hours to fiduciary       |
| Cross-border transfer            | Clause 7        | Permitted unless restricted |
| Audit right                      | Clause 5        | Annual + ad hoc on incident |
| Return / deletion on termination | Clause 8        | 30 days post-termination    |

If your engagement involves sensitive personal data — health, financial,
or biometric — Clause 3 should be replaced with the more stringent
"Reasonable Security Practices" standard prescribed under the IT Act 43A
SPDI Rules until the DPDP Rules supersede them.

## Stamp duty, e-stamping, and execution

Vendor agreements in India attract ad-valorem stamp duty under each
state's Stamp Act. Maharashtra and Karnataka are the highest-volume
states for SaaS vendor contracts and follow these slabs:

- **Maharashtra**: 0.1% of contract value, capped at ₹25,000 (Article 5
  of Schedule I to the Maharashtra Stamp Act, 1958).
- **Karnataka**: 0.1% of contract value, no cap (Article 5(d) of the
  Karnataka Stamp Act, 1957).
- **Delhi**: ₹100 fixed, since vendor agreements are treated as "agreement
  not otherwise provided for" under Article 5(c).
- **Tamil Nadu**: 1% of consideration, with a ceiling of ₹50,000.

For digital execution via DocuSign, Adobe Sign, or eMudhra, the
agreement is enforceable under Section 5 of the IT Act, 2000 — but
e-stamping must be completed separately on the SHCIL portal or the state
e-stamping system. Many counsel paper a "stamping covenant" requiring
the buyer to complete stamping within 7 days of execution; the template
includes this as Clause 23.

## Termination: the practical drafting question

The most-disputed termination clause in Indian vendor agreements is the
**data and asset transition** obligation. Buyers demand a 90-day
transition period at no additional cost; vendors push back because their
team has already been redeployed. The template uses the market-standard
compromise:

- 30-day notice for termination for convenience.
- 90-day transition period during which the vendor provides reasonable
  cooperation at the existing fee rate, with explicit deliverables
  (knowledge transfer, data export, account credentials, runbooks).
- After 90 days, transition support continues only at vendor's then-current
  rate card.

This three-tier structure is clearer than the typical "reasonable
cooperation for a reasonable period at reasonable rates" boilerplate and
courts have favoured the explicit version in disputes.

> **Drafting note:** The template's transition clause specifically lists
> the data formats in which the vendor must export buyer data. If you do
> not specify "machine-readable, non-proprietary formats including CSV,
> JSON, or industry-standard equivalent," vendors have been known to
> deliver data in proprietary formats requiring further licence
> purchases. Insist on the explicit list.

## What this template does not cover

A handful of vendor relationships need bespoke drafting beyond this
template:

- **Public-sector procurement** — government departments and PSUs have
  GFR-mandated contract formats that this template cannot substitute. Use
  the GeM portal templates instead.
- **Construction and EPC contracts** — these need FIDIC-style or
  state-specific PWD frameworks. The template's commercial-services
  drafting is not appropriate.
- **Manufacturing supply** — for ongoing supply of physical goods,
  layer in standard purchase order terms with order acceptance
  mechanics. The template handles services-led engagements better than
  pure goods supply.
- **Cross-border with foreign vendor** — retain MCIA seat or shift to
  Singapore International Arbitration Centre; the template's tax,
  withholding, and FEMA compliance clauses need supplementation.

## Final checklist before signing

Before issuing the agreement to the vendor, verify:

1. The Statement of Work is filled in with measurable deliverables, not
   "general services to be agreed."
2. The Service Level Schedule has numerical thresholds and corresponding
   credits, not "best efforts."
3. The Pricing Schedule specifies invoicing cadence, payment terms, and
   the MSME fallback.
4. The vendor has provided MSME registration certificate or has
   represented non-MSME status in writing.
5. The DPDP Schedule is retained (or expressly waived) and the breach
   notification window is filled in.
6. The arbitration seat, institution, and rules are filled in.
7. Stamp duty is calculated for the state of execution and the
   stamping covenant has a deadline.
8. Both signatories have authority — for companies, board resolution; for
   LLPs, designated partner under the LLP Act, 2008.

A vendor agreement that gets these eight things right takes a week to
negotiate and sign. One that doesn't ends up in arbitration two years
later with the parties arguing what "reasonable" meant.
